Documents/Tell an organisation to stop using your data (right to object)

Documents · Letter · EU, Italy, Germany

Tell an organisation to stop using your data (right to object)

A letter to object to direct marketing, which must stop, or to a use based on the organisation's legitimate interest, which it must justify, with the difference explained.

Also in: Italiano · Deutsch

For

Anyone in the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein or Norway who wants to use this right under the GDPR (the EU General Data Protection Regulation), with notes for Italy and Germany. Also for the data protection officer or small-business owner who receives such a letter.

Not covered

National opt-out registers for telemarketing and the ePrivacy rules on marketing e-mails and calls, which apply alongside the GDPR; withdrawal of consent (Article 7(3)); the UK and Switzerland. The data protection laws of the states other than Bavaria were not read.

Law as of

GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) as published on EUR-Lex, read 7 Nov 2026; EDPB Guidelines 01/2022 on the right of access, version 2.1 (adopted 28 March 2023, minor corrections 30 May 2024); Regulation (EEC, Euratom) 1182/71, Article 3; Italian Data Protection Code (Legislative Decree 196/2003) as on Normattiva (updated to the act published on 19 Feb 2026), read 4 to 8 Nov 2026; Garante complaint page (docweb 4535524), read 7 Nov 2026; BDSG as published on gesetze-im-internet.de, last amended by the Act of 12 May 2026, read 7 Nov 2026; Bavarian Data Protection Act (BayDSG) Articles 15 and 18, text in force from 1 Aug 2025 (gesetze-bayern.de), BDSG section 40(2) and the BfDI pages on the state authorities, read 8 Nov 2026; Court of Justice judgment in Case C-132/21 of 12 January 2023 (EUR-Lex), read 8 Nov 2026

01

What this right is

You can tell an organisation to stop using your data in two kinds of case. The difference matters, because one is absolute and the other is weighed.

QuestionDirect marketingLegitimate interest or public task
WhenYour data is used to market to you, including profiling for that marketingThe organisation relies on its legitimate interests (point (f) of Article 6(1)) or on a public task (point (e))
Do you need a reason?No. "the right to object at any time" (Article 21(2))Yes: "on grounds relating to his or her particular situation" (Article 21(1))
Can the organisation refuse?No. "the personal data shall no longer be processed for such purposes" (Article 21(3))Only if it "demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds for the processing which override the interests, rights and freedoms of the data subject or for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims" (Article 21(1))
CostFree, "at any time and free of charge" (recital 70, one of the GDPR's introductory explanations)Free (Article 12(5))

The organisation must bring this right to your attention "explicitly", at the latest at the first communication, "clearly and separately from any other information" (Article 21(4)). Online, you may object "by automated means using technical specifications" (Article 21(5)). For research and statistics there is a separate rule (Article 21(6)).

While the organisation checks its grounds under Article 21(1), you can ask it to restrict the use of the data (Article 18(1)(d)). If your objection succeeds, you can also ask for erasure (Article 17(1)(c)).

If you gave consent for the use (for example by ticking a box), you can simply withdraw it; that is a different rule (Article 7(3)).

02

Where to send it

Send it to the organisation that decides why and how your data is used. The law calls it the controller, and calls you the data subject. Its contact details are in its privacy notice. Many organisations have a data protection officer: an expert, inside or outside the organisation, whom people can contact about their data. The organisation must "publish the contact details of the data protection officer" (Article 37(7)), and "Data subjects may contact the data protection officer with regard to all issues related to processing of their personal data and to the exercise of their rights" (Article 38(4)). Look for the contact in the privacy notice, often under "data protection officer", "DPO" or "privacy contact".

Use a channel that keeps a record: e-mail, the organisation's own form, or registered post. Keep a copy and note the date it arrived, because the time limit starts then. The organisation "shall facilitate the exercise of data subject rights" (Article 12(2)). If you write by e-mail, the answer comes by e-mail where possible, unless you ask for another way (Article 12(3)).

03

How long the organisation has

The organisation must tell you what it has done "without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt of the request". It may take "two further months where necessary, taking into account the complexity and number of the requests", but it must tell you so within the first month and give the reasons (Article 12(3)).

The European Data Protection Board (EDPB, the body of all EU data protection regulators) counts the month under Regulation 1182/71: a request received on 5 March is due "until and including 5 April"; if the last day "falls on a weekend or a public holiday, the controller has until the next working day to respond" (EDPB Guidelines 01/2022, paras 160 to 161, pp. 50 to 51; Regulation 1182/71, Article 3). Fino's Response timeline counts the dates for you.

04

If no answer comes, or the answer is a refusal

If the organisation decides not to act, it must tell you within one month "the reasons for not taking action" and "the possibility of lodging a complaint with a supervisory authority and seeking a judicial remedy" (Article 12(4)).

Complain to a regulator. A supervisory authority is the public data protection regulator. You may complain "in particular in the Member State of his or her habitual residence, place of work or place of the alleged infringement" (Article 77). Fino's Which regulator? tool and the EDPB list of member authorities give the addresses. Attach your letter, proof of the date it arrived and any reply.

Go to court. You may also bring a claim before the courts of the country where the organisation has an establishment or, unless the organisation is a public authority using its public powers, where you normally live (Article 79). The Court of Justice ruled that a complaint to a regulator and a court action against its decision (Articles 77(1) and 78(1)), on the one hand, and a court claim against the organisation (Article 79(1)), on the other, may be used "concurrently with and independently of each other". It is for each Member State "to lay down detailed rules as regards the relationship between those remedies in order to ensure the effective protection of the rights guaranteed by that regulation and the consistent and homogeneous application of its provisions, as well as the right to an effective remedy before a court or tribunal" (Article 47 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights) (Case C-132/21, ruling). For Italy, see the rules below; whether they fit that ruling is not settled in the sources read for this document. For Germany, the section below gives the competent court.

05

The letter

Fill in the parts in braces, delete the lines that do not apply, and keep a copy. For the reference, use the customer number or account e-mail the organisation knows you by: it helps them find your data without asking for more.

Keep Option A or Option B and delete the other. The last sentence, asking for erasure, is optional.

How the organisation handles it: Handling data requests: procedure and register. Its replies: confirm receipt, confirm identity, extend the time limit, refuse or charge a fee, the answer.

What the organisation may ask or refuse

Article 12 allows three things. Check who you are, if it has "reasonable doubts concerning the identity of the natural person making the request", and then only for "additional information necessary to confirm the identity" (Article 12(6)). Refuse, or charge a fee, only if a request is "manifestly unfounded or excessive, in particular because of their repetitive character"; the organisation "shall bear the burden of demonstrating" this. Otherwise the answer is "free of charge" (Article 12(5)). Take longer: up to two further months for complex or numerous requests, if it tells you within the first month and gives reasons (Article 12(3)). The law of a Member State may also limit a right (Article 23): see Italy and Germany below.

Fined, and Article 21 among the breaches

Banco Bilbao Vizcaya Argentaria S.A., Garante (Italy), 3 September 2026, €5,508,000: in-app marketing notifications continued after the customer switched them off and told customer service; Articles 12, 21 and 24 among the breaches (2026/IT/106). Delivery Hero Germany GmbH, Data Protection Authority of Berlin, August 2019, fines totalling €195,407 including fees, final: advertising e-mails were sent despite objections, among other breaches of people's rights; Articles 15, 17 and 21 among the rules involved, part of the fines set under the law in force before the GDPR (2019/DE/018). Each fine covers all the breaches found, not this article alone. Amounts, dates and regulators as on Fino's decision pages.

Italy

Italy

Complaining in Italy

The Italian regulator is the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali (the Garante). A complaint (reclamo) is made under Article 77 GDPR and Articles 140-bis to 143 of the Italian Data Protection Code (Codice in materia di protezione dei dati personali, Legislative Decree 196/2003; here, the Code). The Garante says it is free: "La presentazione del reclamo è gratuita" (Garante, how to act). It can be sent by certified e-mail (PEC, posta elettronica certificata) to protocollo@pec.gpdp.it, by registered post with return receipt, or by hand, to Piazza Venezia 11, 00187 Rome. It is signed with a certified or digital signature, or by hand with a copy of a valid identity document attached (Garante, complaint model and instructions).

The complaint sets out the facts, the rules thought to be breached, the measures asked for and who the controller is (Code, Article 142). The Garante "decide il reclamo entro nove mesi dalla data di presentazione" (decides within nine months) and reports on progress within three months; with reasons given, it may take twelve months (Code, Article 143(3)).

Complaint or court. The person may complain to the Garante or go to court. A complaint "non può essere proposto" (cannot be made) if a court already has the same matter between the same parties; and once a complaint is made, a court claim between the same parties on the same matter is not admissible, except as Article 10(4) of Legislative Decree 150/2011 provides (Code, Article 140-bis). Court claims go to the ordinary courts (Code, Article 152).

When Italian law limits the right

The rights in Articles 15 to 22 GDPR cannot be used, by request or by complaint, where this could cause "un pregiudizio effettivo e concreto" (an actual and concrete harm) to interests the Code lists: anti-money-laundering rules; support for victims of extortion; parliamentary committees of inquiry; certain public monetary and financial supervision; defence investigations or the exercise of a right in court; the confidentiality of a whistleblower's identity; tax interests and the fight against tax evasion (Code, Article 2-undecies(1)). In most of these cases the controller must send a reasoned notice without delay, unless the notice would defeat the purpose of the limit, and the rights may then be exercised through the Garante (Article 2-undecies(3)).

Rights of a person who has died

For a deceased person, the rights in Articles 15 to 22 "possono essere esercitati da chi ha un interesse proprio, o agisce a tutela dell'interessato, in qualità di suo mandatario, o per ragioni familiari meritevoli di protezione" (may be exercised by someone with an interest of their own, acting to protect the person as their agent, or for family reasons worth protecting). This is excluded where a law says so, or where the person forbade it in writing to the controller, the latter only for information society services offered directly to them. Such a ban cannot harm third parties' property rights arising from the death or their right to defend their interests in court (Code, Article 2-terdecies).

If you write about a deceased person, add a line on your link to them (heir, agent, family member) and attach proof.

Germany

Germany

Complaining in Germany

Germany has a data protection authority in each federal state (Land), and two in Bavaria: the Bavarian State Office for Data Protection Supervision (Landesamt für Datenschutzaufsicht, in Ansbach) for companies and other private bodies, and the Bavarian State Commissioner for Data Protection (Landesbeauftragter für den Datenschutz) for Bavarian public bodies, municipalities included (Article 15(1) and Article 18(1) and (2) of the Bavarian Data Protection Act, BayDSG; BfDI page on Bavaria). For private companies it is in principle the authority of the state where the company is established: "Die nach Landesrecht zuständigen Behörden überwachen ... bei den nichtöffentlichen Stellen die Anwendung der Vorschriften über den Datenschutz" (section 40(1) BDSG; the BDSG is the Federal Data Protection Act, Bundesdatenschutzgesetz). If the company has several establishments in Germany, the authority is the one of its main establishment, as the GDPR defines it (section 40(2) BDSG; Article 4(16)). According to the BfDI, the state authority supervises the state and municipal bodies of its state: "Neben der Aufsicht über die Landes- und Kommunalbehörden wird auch der nicht-öffentliche Bereich grundsätzlich von den Landesdatenschutzbehörden betreut" (besides supervising state and municipal authorities, the state data protection authorities in principle also cover the private sector) (BfDI: contact to the state authorities), which lists them with their addresses. Federal public bodies, and telecommunications companies within the limits of section 9, are supervised by the Federal Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (BfDI) (section 9(1) BDSG). An authority that receives a complaint for which another authority is competent passes it on (section 19(2) BDSG).

Court. A claim against a company may be brought at the court of a place where it has an establishment, or where the person normally lives (section 44(1) BDSG). This does not apply to claims against authorities using their public powers (section 44(2)).

When German law limits the right

Section 36 BDSG applies only to public bodies: the right to object under Article 21(1) does not exist against a public body where a compelling public interest outweighs the person's interests, or where a law requires the processing (section 36 BDSG). It does not limit objections to direct marketing.

Section 27(2) limits the right under Article 21 for research and statistics where it is likely to make those purposes impossible or seriously impair them, and the limit is necessary for those research or statistics purposes (section 27(2) BDSG).

For archiving in the public interest, the right in Article 21 does not apply to the extent that it is likely to make those purposes impossible or seriously impair them, and the exceptions are necessary for those purposes (section 28(4) BDSG).
