Explainer No. 4 · AI and new tech · Updated 19 Oct 2026 · 3 min read

We’re training an AI model on customer data. Has anyone been fined for that?

Yes, and the best-known case also shows why you should check what happened next. Italy’s regulator fined OpenAI €15 million in a decision of 2 November 2024, for training ChatGPT on personal data without a proper legal basis and without telling people enough. In March 2026 the Court of Rome upheld OpenAI’s challenge and annulled the decision, and the regulator took it off its website. That fine no longer stands. Clearview AI was fined €20 million or more in several countries for building a face-recognition database from photos taken from the web; the Dutch fine of €30.5 million is final. The key question for you: your customers gave you their data for the service, and training a model is usually a new purpose. You must be able to justify it, tell them, and let them object.

Training is usually a new purpose

Article 5(1)(b) says data collected for one purpose must

“not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes”

Article 6(4) gives the test for whether a new purpose is compatible: how close the two purposes are, the context in which you collected the data and your relationship with them, how sensitive the data is, the possible consequences for them, and safeguards such as encryption or pseudonymisation (replacing names with codes).

Legitimate interests, with a written test

Most companies rely on legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)). That can work for AI training, but only after a documented three-step test: a real interest, training that is necessary for it, and a balance against people’s rights that comes out in your favour. What people would reasonably expect weighs heavily. The European Data Protection Board explained how to apply the test to AI models in December 2024. EDPB Opinion 28/2024 ↗

Tell customers, and let them object

Customers must be told about the training (Article 13). When you rely on legitimate interests, they have the right to object to it at any time, on grounds relating to their particular situation (Article 21(1)). Article 21(4) says this right must be brought to their attention explicitly, “at the latest at the time of the first communication”, and “presented clearly and separately from any other information”.

Italy’s Character.AI decision of July 2026 shows how this is enforced. The regulator fined the company because people in Italy were not properly told that their data was used to pre-train its models (Article 14), and because its privacy information did not explain the right to object clearly. It did not find a breach of Article 21 itself: it said an opt-out offered before training, as the European Data Protection Board recommends, could not be required before 17 December 2024, the date of the Board’s Opinion 28/2024.

Sensitive data and the impact assessment

Health, biometric or similar data in the training set falls under Article 9, with much stricter rules. Training on large amounts of customer data with new technology is likely to be high risk (Article 35(1)), so you are likely to need a written impact assessment first.

Where the AI Act comes in

The AI Act adds its own rules on top of the GDPR. Providers of general-purpose AI models must publish a summary of their training content (Article 53(1)(d), since August 2025). High-risk AI systems will need data-quality controls (Article 10) from December 2027, or August 2028 for AI built into regulated products, after the 2026 amendment (Article 113). The full AI Act, as amended, is on Fino.

Check before you train

  1. Did customers know, when they gave you their data, that it might train models?
  2. Is the right to object explained clearly and separately, and can they use it easily? The EDPB recommends letting them opt out before the training happens.
  3. Do you need personal data at all, or would anonymised or pseudonymised data do?
  4. Is there health, biometric or children’s data in the set?
  5. Is there a written impact assessment and a legitimate interests assessment?
